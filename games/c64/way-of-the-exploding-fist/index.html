Commodore 64 · 1985 · Melbourne House

Way of the Exploding Fist

Two karateka of nine sprites each, assembled pose by pose from 255 cut-out pieces, scored by how far apart they stand when a blow lands.

01 · The fighters

Every pose is built from nine pieces

A fighter is a three-by-three grid of multicolour sprites. The game never stores a whole pose. Each shape frame is nine cell numbers, and each cell is a 64-byte piece of a karateka.

Stick

Press a direction, with or without fire, to play that move's animation as the game stores it: a list of steps, each a number of ticks, a shape frame and a step in X and Y ($7720). The pose is assembled the way the sprite builder ($3914) does it, from the frame tables at $7AE0 and the cells at $8000; facing left takes the columns in reverse and mirrors every byte through the table at $7E00 ($3BC5). Checked against both fighters' sprites in a snapshot of play.

Sixteen stick positions give sixteen moves, in the table at $286C. Down-forward from standing is a punch (move $18), and from a crouch a different blow (move 7); fire with back from standing becomes a turn round ($12). There are 79 shape frames. The karateka use $00-$3A, and $3B-$4C are the bull of the bonus round, which uses the same tables but takes each byte as a sprite pointer instead of a cell ($5D = $40).

Each fighter has two 576-byte buffers at $C040-$C93F and is rebuilt into the spare one whenever its frame changes, so a half-built pose is never on screen. A cell of 0 leaves its sprite blank: the standing pose fills six of the nine.

02 · The screen

Seven raster interrupts draw one frame

Two fighters need eighteen sprites and the machine has eight. The game draws the top row of both fighters with sprites 0-5, then moves those sprites down the screen as the beam passes.

One frame of a two-player bout, drawn from memory and the recorded writes to the video chip with the site's renderer: all 104,448 pixels match the emulator's own picture of the same frame. The chain: $31F2 at line 0 sets up the frame, $2FB0 switches to the bitmap at line 90, the sprite handlers move sprites 0-5 down a row of 21 lines at a time, a handler on line 217 draws the shadow, and $30E5 at line 255 returns to text for the panel and plays the music.

The gap between the two fighters' heights decides which of seven interrupt chains runs that frame ($32CD). When one fighter is exactly 20 lines higher than the other, one fighter's second row and the other's third start a line apart, and a combined handler moves all six sprites at once ($2F02, $2F59). The routines that set this up ($3291-$3750) work by rewriting the handlers' own instructions: the raster line, the sprite heights and the pointers are all operands patched every frame.

A jumping fighter casts a shadow on the floor. It is a fourth row of sprites from a fixed set under the I/O chips ($D480), shown at a fixed height while the fighter is off the ground.

03 · The backdrops

Four pictures in 13 kilobytes

The backdrop changes every stage, and the disk is never read after loading. All four pictures sit in memory packed, and are unpacked into the bitmap when the stage begins.

Unpacked on this page from the game's bytes by ports of bitmap_unpack ($17BB) and colour_unpack ($1849); for the first backdrop the result matches the snapshot's bitmap and screen colours byte for byte. The slider replays the bitmap writes in the order the game makes them.

The bitmap is packed as runs, read from the end of the stream backwards and written from the bottom of the picture up ($F53F down to $E640): a control byte below $80 copies that many bytes, one above repeats a single byte. The colours are packed separately as four-bit values, in three passes over the 480 colour cells. Each picture covers character rows 5 to 16, the band between the score panel and the floor.

The floor below never changes. Its colours are all set to yellow ($192F), so whatever the bitmap holds there is invisible, and the game keeps its speech samples in that hidden part of the bitmap ($F540-$FFDC).

04 · Music

Music: four tunes, tuned for an American machine

The attract mode, the one-player game, the two-player game and the high-score table each have a tune, played on three voices by a driver called once a frame from the raster interrupt at line 255.

The game's own driver ($09A5) running in a 6502 interpreter on this page, over the music bytes as the game loads them; its writes to the sound chip match the game's code run in a simulator, every register of every frame for 3,000 frames of each tune. Played through the site's model of the SID, filter on (the cutoff is one 6581's). Each voice shows the note the tune asks for and, under Frequency, the note a PAL machine actually plays.

Each voice follows its own order list of patterns, with commands to play a pattern, jump and repeat ($0D31). There are 25 instruments, with pulse-width and filter sweeps driven by three slow oscillators and by voice 3's output read back from the chip ($0DFC).

The note table ($0D14) gives each semitone's frequency for the clock of an NTSC machine: every note is within 5 cents of pitch at 1,022,727 Hz. A PAL Commodore 64 runs at 985,248 Hz, so on this PAL release every note sounds 65 to 70 cents flat, two-thirds of a semitone.

05 · Sound

Sound: the shouts are played by switching the volume on and off

The grunts and cries of the fight are recordings, played while the music runs. The sound chip has no way to play a sample, so the game flips its master volume between full and silent thousands of times a second.

Each sample byte picks a delay from a 256-entry table ($0200/$0300), and when the delay runs out a timer interrupt flips the volume ($311A). Played here as those flips, with the delay measured in the emulator: 9 cycles per table unit plus 108. The raster interrupts that delay the timer on the real machine are not reproduced.

The volume register also sets the music's loudness, so the samples ride on top of the tune and the tune briefly disappears while the volume is at zero. Sample 2 is played with every attack ($2B54), the others with the reactions to a blow. DEL in the attract mode turns them off ($CF); the menu calls them sound effects.

06 · Scoring

A blow scores by distance, not timing

The manual talks of well-executed and loosely timed blows. In the code a blow is tested once, on its striking frame, and the only question is how far apart the fighters stand.

A port of the hit test ($2B8E) for fighters facing each other, checked against the game's own routine for every blow, every defender frame and every distance from -40 to 60: 65,549 cases, no difference. Green is a whole point, amber a half, grey a miss; the distance is the attacker's along its facing.

Each blow has a reach profile, one byte per frame the defender might be in ($7200-$7711, 22 profiles). The byte is the distance at which the blow lands squarely. At that distance, or closer by up to a margin the blow sets ($11BD), it is a whole point; closer still, by up to a second margin ($11D6), a half point. A profile byte of $80 means a defender in that frame cannot be hit, which is how jumps and somersaults evade.

Four half points win a bout. Points for the score come from a table in hundreds ($12A5), halved for a half point.

07 · The opponent

Ten dans of the same opponent, with faster reflexes

The computer fights every bout with the same rules. What changes with rank is a set of eight masks that limit how long it waits, walks, holds a move and reacts.

The per-level tables at $27A9-$27FC, as ai_level_setup ($2593) loads them. Each is a mask the computer applies to a random number: for the timers (hold, wait, walk, pause, reaction) a smaller mask means shorter waits; the others narrow its choices. Level 0 is Novice; the level rises by one with each bout won.

When attacked, the computer either blocks with the right block for the blow ($118F), starts one of nine scripted plans ($2774), or counter-attacks. When close it picks an attack from a table indexed by distance ($26D5), and against a blocking player it chooses among the blows that block does not stop ($27A1). The reaction-delay mask is zero from level 4 up, so from 4th dan the computer reacts at once. The walking table has 11 entries for 12 levels, so level 11 reads the first byte of the next table.

In a one-player game, two bout wins clear a rank, from Novice to 10th dan ($1BA7); a lost bout ends the game. Past 10th dan the rank stays on screen as 10TH DAN while the opponent's level is drawn at random from 7 to 10 ($1C65).

08 · The bonus round

Secret: only one blow fells the bull

After a rank is cleared in front of the Buddha, a bull charges across the screen. One of the player's moves, at one distance, fells it.

The bull's frames drawn from its own tables (shape frames $3B-$4C, animations $1C-$1F), its sprites shown directly by pointer from memory nobody else uses: past the fighters' buffers at $C940, under the I/O chips and in unused characters of the font. Right: the emulator, after felling the bull at a distance of 26.

bull_round ($132C) fells the bull only when the player is in move 7, the down-forward blow from a crouch, on its striking frame $0D, with the bull between 25 and 29 units ahead ($136C-$1381). That is worth 3,000 points. Any other blow, or the right blow at the wrong distance, does nothing, and the bull runs the player over. If the bull passes without touching the player, the round ends with 500 points ($1406).

The crouch matters: from standing, down-forward is a different move ($18), so the player has to be crouching already when the bull comes into range. In the emulator, holding down and adding forward when the bull was 28 units away felled it at 26.

09 · Oddities

Bug: a blow can crash the game if a timer is running

After every scoring blow the game reads a register it never sets, and if that register is not zero it throws its own stack out of balance.

The reaction to a blow ($2CA8) reads the control register of CIA 2's timer A ($DD0E). The game never starts that timer, so on a normal machine the read gives 0 and nothing happens. If it is running, the routine pushes two bytes and jumps into the animator, and the return address is lost. In the emulator, with the timer started by hand before a blow, the screen filled with garbage and the processor ended up executing the speech table at $0201. Why the check is there is not known; a cartridge or tool left running in the background is one thing it would catch.

Smaller things found in the code:

  • The 30 seconds on the clock are 30 units of 50 passes of the fight loop ($212F). A pass usually takes one frame, but measured in the emulator a unit took 50 to 66 frames, so a bout lasts 30 to 40 real seconds.
  • The points tie-break at time up ($2169) reads the first fighter's half-or-whole flag for both fighters.
  • RESTORE does nothing: the game's NMI handler ignores any interrupt that did not come from its own timer ($2FF2).
  • Fire on joystick port 1 starts a one-player game from the attract mode, because the function-key scan reads the port the joystick shares with the keyboard ($1E64).
  • Holding fire at the end of a one-player game skips the high-score table, and the new entry keeps the name ...... ($1472).
  • The judge drawn into each backdrop raises a flag for the winner with two extra sprites ($1F93), placed per backdrop.
