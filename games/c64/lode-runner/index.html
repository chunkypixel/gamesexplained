Commodore 64 · 1983 · Brøderbund

Lode Runner

Rooms, guards, and temporary holes in the Commodore 64 disk game.

01 · Room data

The rooms on disk

The maps show actors in their starting cells. Yellow outlines mark hidden exits; blue outlines mark trapdoors. These editor markers disappear during play. Both board sets are drawn with disk artwork; matching boards contain identical stored cells.

Each disk board packs its 28 × 16 cells into 224 bytes. The disk reader skips raw sector byte zero and loads those cells from raw offsets 1–224. Each byte holds two cells, with the low nibble read first. Values above nine become blank when the game builds the room.

The 150 boards occupy tracks 3–12. Those room sectors are identical in the three supplied disks.

Surplus guard and exit markers disappear when a room is built. The disk engine keeps at most five guards and 45 hidden exit cells, even when the stored board contains more. The scan runs from the bottom-right cell toward the top-left, so the first markers encountered get the available slots. $6FA6, $728F, $709C.

Trapdoors and direction

A trapdoor looks like a brick and lets the runner fall through from above. The cell blocks sideways and upward entry. Its picture uses brick tile 1, while the maps keep trapdoor tile 5. $733E, $73C7, $74DE, $7550, $75C8.

The controls show whether a tile supports a centered runner or permits entry from the chosen direction. Climbing also depends on ladder access and movement phase.

02 · Graphics

Bitmap scenery and sprites

Loading the shared C64 renderer.

VICE reference picture

The runner and guards use up to six hardware sprites, in slots 0, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 7. Those sprites move over multicolour bitmap scenery.

The work bitmap at $4000 preserves the scenery. When a shape is erased from the display bitmap at $2000, that background restores the picture beneath the shape.

Each actor’s shape comes from the glyph atlas used for terrain and text. A glyph is eleven rows high, with two encoded patterns supplying each row. Four pairs of lookup pages shift the shape to match the actor’s horizontal phase. $8AF8, $8BF3.

The disk atlas holds 104 shapes as 22 transposed rows of 104 bytes at $A800. Digits occupy indexes 59–68; letters occupy 69–94. These glyphs draw the game’s text directly in the bitmap. The cartridge atlas shows its own 76 compact source shapes, expanded into RAM at startup. A glyph index belongs to the selected atlas.

03 · Digging

When a hole closes

The controls advance the hole’s timer through its two closing pictures. The timer matches the original update routine for every value from 0 to 180.

Drilling opens a brick diagonally below the runner. The cell beside the runner must be clear. Concrete blocks resist drilling. $76E6, $77A5.

A completed hole stays open for 180 main-loop passes. Partial refill pictures appear with 20 and 10 passes remaining. At zero, the brick returns. $7B12, $84F1.

Trapping a guard earns 75 points. Burying the trapped guard when the brick returns earns another 75.

A buried guard moves to a respawn position. After the respawn delay, the guard’s sprite returns when that cell is clear in the actor map. $7BD2, $862F.

At the disk version’s default pace, an undelayed hole lasts about six seconds. The measured PAL setup runs about 30 main-loop passes per second. Scoring, drawing, and disk operations can delay those passes, so the timer follows the game’s progress rather than elapsed seconds.

Digging cadence and hole count

The disk’s normal digging cadence limits how many timed holes can overlap. A successful dig takes 13 runner updates, and each continuing main-loop pass also ages the holes. With legal positions and the normal loop and room-reset flow, a 180-pass lifetime allows at most 14 active timed holes: 180 / 13, rounded up. Walking and interrupted digs lengthen the gap between completions. The allocator has room for 30. $611C, $76E6, $77A5, $7B12.

The final drill check

The neighboring cell is checked on every dig update. A nonblank cell, including a guard marker, cancels the dig even on its final update. The brick remains, and no hole timer is allocated. $7712, $77D3.

04 · Guard movement

Guard route costs

The controls score one candidate exit; route searches remain outside the diagram. The scoring helper matches $81CE for all 200,704 legal row/column combinations.

Guards first try a supported horizontal chase when they share the runner’s row. Otherwise they look for ladders and drop points within their walking range.

The guard assigns a cost to each candidate exit. A candidate on the runner’s row costs the lateral distance from guard to candidate. An exit above the runner costs its vertical gap plus 100; an exit below costs its gap plus 200.

Equal route costs preserve the guard’s search order. The guard tries its current column before searching left and then right. Each side scan starts at its outer walking limit and works inward. A candidate replaces the previous choice only when its cost is strictly lower. $7FD2, $809C, $8106, $816F.

05 · Points

Gold and points

The counter arithmetic and displayed digits match $87E6 in 1,624 boundary and seeded cases.

The status bar hides the score’s highest digit. The counter stores eight decimal digits in four packed-BCD bytes, while the status bar prints the lowest seven. The highest digit remains in the counter and high-score record. Overflow beyond eight digits discards the carry.

Gold is collected only when both movement phases are centered. Collecting the last gold queues a motif and enables hidden exits. $7A4A.

An enabled exit can become a ladder beneath an actor while preserving that actor’s collision marker. With the gold collected, reaching row zero at the centered vertical phase completes the room and awards 1,500 points plus a life. $8D96, $611C, $6162.

06 · Sound

Music: the gold motifs

Loading the shared SID player.

Collecting the last gold queues one of ten short motifs. A countdown chooses the motif. A separate pitch offset changes after ten completed rooms, cycles through 2–11, and then returns to 2. $6410, $641D, $643A.

The queued music uses SID voices two and three with the original frequency tables. A negative harmony byte detunes the third voice by subtracting 128 from the second voice’s frequency word. $9521, $9546.

The stored envelope bytes do not change the queue’s sustain/release setting. The player reads each event’s fourth byte and then replaces that byte with a fixed value. $6319.

High pitch offsets can reach beyond the 37 stored frequency words. The “High transpose” button plays the adjacent bytes that the driver then reads. Some produce very low frequencies that laptop and phone speakers barely reproduce.

Playback uses the site’s PAL SID model. The game’s roughly 60 Hz CIA queue updates are grouped into video frames. Gameplay effects on voice one and timing within a video frame are omitted.

Commands labeled Ctrl also accept other modifier combinations with the same letter. The IRQ sets the key byte’s high bit whenever a modifier is active, matching the command codes at $7A10.

The editor reads and writes the same packed sectors used by the game. Its commands play, clear, edit, move, initialize, and clear scores. $65D6.

The editor protects the master disk’s room data. Clearing the master score file is permitted. $6A88.

High scores rank displayed level before points. Before writing an edited record, the game rereads the score sector. $6CAD.

08 · Editions

Seventeen rooms in the cartridge

The supplied cartridge has 17 boards that match rooms in the disk’s 150-board set. Their order jumps across that set: the cartridge’s third board is disk room 111. Select an edition in the room browser to follow its sequence.

Cartridge boards are stored as runs of repeated cells. A descriptor puts the tile ID in its low nibble and the run length minus one in its high nibble. Each board expands to the same 448 cells used on disk, matching its corresponding disk board exactly.

The cartridge’s default main loop waits for three timer interrupts per undelayed pass. The disk version waits for two.

Both engines use the same guard route-cost rule and score arithmetic, including the hidden eighth score digit.

The engines have separate addresses, room stores, graphics, and sound directories. The Source tab covers the disk engine. Cartridge addresses belong to the separate engine documented in the edition evidence.
